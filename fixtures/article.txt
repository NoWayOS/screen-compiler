The river path runs for eleven kilometres from the old mill to the estuary, and on most days you can walk the whole of it without crossing a road. It was laid out more than a century ago as a towpath, when barges still carried grain and timber down to the harbour, and much of the original stone edging survives. Today it is a place for walking, running and cycling slowly, and for sitting on one of the forty benches set along the bank.

Before you set out, check the tide table at the visitor point by the mill. Below the lock gates the lower stretch floods at the highest spring tides, usually for an hour either side of high water. When it does, a signed route takes you up through the woods and back down to the bank at the footbridge; it adds about twenty minutes and a short climb.

The first section, from the mill to the lock, is the busiest. The surface is smooth and level, and there are toilets, a water fountain and a small cafe in the lock keeper's cottage, open from nine until five. Look out for the heron that fishes below the weir most mornings; it has become so used to people that it will often stand within a few metres of the path.

Between the lock and the footbridge the path narrows and runs under a canopy of alder and willow. In late spring the banks are thick with wild garlic and the air smells of it for days. This is the quietest part of the walk, and in the early morning you may see kingfishers, a flash of blue skimming low over the water, or hear the plop of a water vole dropping in from the far bank. Please keep dogs on a lead here between March and July, when birds are nesting in the reeds.

The footbridge itself was rebuilt in steel and oak twelve years ago after the old timber bridge was washed away in a winter flood. From its centre there is a long view downstream to the hills, and on a clear evening the sun sets almost exactly along the line of the river. A plaque at the eastern end records the height the water reached that winter; it is well above head height, and a reminder of how quickly the river can change.

Beyond the bridge the valley opens out. The path follows the edge of the water meadows, which are grazed in summer by a small herd of cattle. They are used to walkers and will usually ignore you, but give them room, never walk between a cow and her calf, and close every gate behind you. In wet weather this stretch can be muddy; sturdy shoes are a better choice than trainers.

Halfway along the meadows a boardwalk crosses the old oxbow, a curve of the river cut off when the main channel moved two hundred years ago. It is now a shallow pond fringed with reeds and is one of the best places in the valley for dragonflies. On a warm afternoon in July you may count a dozen kinds without leaving the boardwalk. There is a hide at the far end, with information boards that help you name what you see.

The last three kilometres follow the sea wall to the estuary. The views here are wide and the light changes constantly as cloud moves over the mudflats. In winter thousands of wading birds feed on the mud at low tide: curlew, redshank, dunlin and godwit, rising together in great twisting flocks when something disturbs them. Please stay on the path along the top of the wall and keep dogs close, so that the birds can feed undisturbed through the short winter days.

The path ends at the estuary car park, where a bus runs back to the mill every hour until early evening. Timetables are posted at both stops. If you would rather walk back, allow about three hours for the return at an easy pace, more if you stop to watch the birds.

Looking after the path

The path is looked after by a small team of rangers and more than a hundred volunteers, who clear fallen branches, repair the stone edging, cut back the brambles and count the birds each month. If you would like to help, you can join a work party on the first Saturday of every month; tools, gloves and a hot drink are provided, and no experience is needed.

You can help in smaller ways too. Take your litter home with you, including fruit peel, which takes far longer to break down than most people think. Keep to the path where the banks are soft, so that they do not wear away. Leave flowers and plants for others to enjoy. If you see something that needs attention, such as a fallen tree, a broken gate or a sign that has been damaged, report it at the visitor point or leave a note in the box at the lock keeper's cottage.

Accessibility

The section from the mill to the lock is level, firm and wide enough for wheelchairs and pushchairs, with resting places every two hundred metres. Beyond the lock the path is narrower and uneven in places, and the boardwalk across the oxbow has a step at each end. An accessible route to the estuary viewpoint starts from the estuary car park, where there is level parking close to the sea wall and a ramp up to the path. Large print and audio guides to the walk can be borrowed free of charge from the visitor point.

Through the year

Every season brings something different to the river. In spring the willows come into leaf, the first swallows arrive over the meadows and the banks fill with flowers. Summer is the time for dragonflies, butterflies and long, light evenings, when the path stays busy until late. In autumn the alders turn gold, salmon can sometimes be seen leaping at the weir, and the first flocks of geese arrive on the estuary from the north. Winter is the season for the estuary birds, for frost on the meadows and for the clear, low light that makes the whole valley glow in the late afternoon.

Whatever the season, the walk rewards those who take it slowly. There is no need to reach the estuary; many people walk as far as the footbridge and back, or simply sit for an hour by the weir. However far you go, we hope you enjoy your time by the river, and that you will come back to see it change.

The mill

The walk begins at the old mill, a tall brick building with a slate roof that stands where the river leaves the town. Grain was ground here for nearly three hundred years, first by water and later by steam, until the mill closed in the years after the last war. For a long time it stood empty, its windows boarded and its wheel rusting in the race. It was restored by a local trust and now houses the visitor point, a small museum and a workshop where you can watch a miller grind flour on the restored stones on the second Sunday of each month.

The museum tells the story of the river and the people who worked on it: the millers and their families, the bargemen who carried the flour down to the harbour, and the lock keepers who lived beside the water and kept the gates in order. There are photographs, tools, ledgers recording every load that passed, and a model of the valley as it was before the railway came. Entry is free, though donations help to keep the building open.

Getting there

The mill is a ten-minute walk from the railway station; follow the signs for the river from the main entrance and turn left at the bridge. Buses from the town centre stop outside the mill gates every twenty minutes during the day. There is a small car park beside the mill, but it fills early on fine weekends, and the larger car park by the leisure centre is only five minutes' walk away. Bicycles can be left in the covered racks at the visitor point.

If you plan to walk one way and take the bus back, it is usually easier to start at the estuary and walk upstream, so that you finish at the mill with the cafe and the station close at hand. Either way, check the times of the last buses before you set out, especially in winter, when the service ends earlier.

Safety by the water

The river looks gentle, but it can be cold and fast, and its banks are steep in places. Keep children close, particularly near the weir and the lock, where the water runs deep and quick. Do not swim in the river or the oxbow; there are hidden channels, cold currents and weed that can tangle a swimmer's legs. Life rings hang on posts at the lock, the footbridge and the sea wall; know where the nearest one is as you walk.

In poor weather the exposed sea wall can be windy and wet, and the light fades quickly on winter afternoons. Carry a waterproof layer, wear shoes with a good grip, and let someone know where you are going if you are walking alone. Mobile phone coverage is good along most of the path but patchy in the wooded section below the lock.

A note on the seasons for walkers

The path is open every day of the year. After heavy rain the meadows and the woodland section can be very muddy, and occasionally the lower path is closed for repairs; notices are posted at both ends and at the visitor point when it is. In the hottest weeks of summer, carry water, as there is no fountain beyond the lock, and the open meadows and sea wall offer little shade.

However you choose to walk it, the river path is a shared space. Cyclists are asked to ride slowly, ring their bells well before passing and give way to walkers, and walkers to keep to one side when a bicycle approaches. Runners are welcome at any hour. A little care from everyone keeps the path a calm and pleasant place for all who use it.

Thank you for visiting. The rangers at the visitor point are glad to answer questions about the walk, the wildlife or the history of the valley, and to hear what you saw along the way. Many of the best sightings recorded in the logbook by the door were made by visitors on their first walk: an otter at dusk below the footbridge, a hobby hunting dragonflies over the oxbow, a seal resting on the mud at the mouth of the estuary. Perhaps your name will be in it next.

Opening hours for the visitor point and museum are from nine until five every day but the twenty-fifth of December; the path itself never closes, and the benches along it are there for anyone who needs a rest, a view or a few quiet minutes by the water.

Dogs are welcome on the whole of the path; bowls of water are left out by the cafe door on warm days.
